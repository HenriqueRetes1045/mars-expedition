MISSION CONTROL: The Mars Expedition

Mission briefing · MARS-01

Two explorers. One rover. A planet full of decisions.

Work together to plan a robotic expedition to Mars. Every major decision changes your resources and your story. Explain the science behind your choices to build your own mission log.

Mission: Collect valuable scientific evidence while keeping your spacecraft operational.

How to play: Read the briefing together, inspect the scientific evidence, discuss possible actions, and write a justification before confirming each choice. You will swap roles after the journey to Mars. Your grade should reflect your reasoning, not whether you achieve a perfect ending.
Approximately 40–60 minutes · Offline · One device per pair

Mission debrief · Expedition complete

Assessment reminder: A difficult ending is not automatically a poor assessment. Scientific reasoning, justified trade-offs and reflection are what matter.

Final team reflection

Discuss and write your answers together. These will be added to your mission report.

On an iPad, use the browser's Print / Share options to save a PDF. The downloadable HTML report is also useful if printing is unavailable. Always export your report before clearing browser data.

Teacher assessment guidance

Suggested criteria: Knowledge and Understanding 25%, Critical Thinking 35%, Communication 20%, Application 20%. Review explanations in context rather than grading the story's ending or resource totals alone.

Scientific reference library

Use these figures to support your arguments. The game expects you to interpret evidence, not simply guess.

GravityEarth: 9.8 N/kg · Mars: 3.7 N/kg

An object's mass does not change between planets, but its weight depends on gravitational field strength.

Mars's atmosphereVery thin · Mostly CO₂

Surface pressure is less than 1% of Earth's average sea-level pressure, so parachutes alone cannot safely land a heavy rover.

Surface temperatureAbout −63 °C on average

Temperatures vary widely. Machines need insulation and thermal protection.

Solar energyLess sunlight than Earth

Mars receives about 43% of the sunlight Earth receives at the top of the atmosphere. Dust can reduce power further.

Travel and communicationOne-way delay: minutes

Radio signals travel at light speed, but the distance to Earth changes. Robots must make some decisions autonomously.

Rock and iceClues about past water

Layered sedimentary rocks and water ice can help scientists investigate Mars's history. They do not, by themselves, prove that life exists.

Values are rounded for Year 6; specific environmental conditions vary by place and season.
